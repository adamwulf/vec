Good evening, and thank you all for coming out to the field station on a damp night like this one. Tonight I want to walk you through the ecology of a temperate tidal freshwater marsh, and rather than speak in the abstract I will keep returning to one site as our running example, the Marsh at Cormorant Bend, which sits where the Alder River finally slows and spreads out just before it reaches the sea. Almost everything I describe tonight is drawn from field seasons at that single marsh.

The most important variable in any wetland, the one you must understand before anything else makes sense, is water. More precisely it is the hydroperiod, which is the pattern of how deep the water sits and for how long it stays. At Cormorant Bend the marsh floods and drains twice a day on the tide, and yet the water there stays essentially fresh, because the steady discharge of the Alder River holds the salt wedge from the sea penned downstream. We call a marsh like that oligohaline, meaning only very lightly salty, usually under half a part per thousand, and that low salinity shapes every living thing in it.

Let me introduce you to the plants, because the plants are the architecture of the place. The species that defines the low marsh at Cormorant Bend is Lyngbye's sedge, a tall grasslike plant with sharp triangular stems that forms stands so dense you can barely push through them by the middle of August. Just a little higher up, on ground that floods less often, the broadleaf cattail takes over, with its familiar brown cigar shaped seed spikes standing above the water. The boundary between the sedge and the cattail is not random. It tracks the elevation of the ground almost to the centimeter, because each species can only tolerate so many hours a day with its roots underwater.

Now here is the part that genuinely surprises people the first time they hear it. These marsh plants grow furiously through the summer, die back every winter, and yet their dead stems and roots do not fully rot away, because the waterlogged soil holds almost no oxygen for the decomposers to breathe. Year after year that half decayed plant material simply piles up in place as a spongy dark soil we call peat. At Cormorant Bend the peat is now nearly two meters deep in the oldest part of the marsh, and it is still building upward at roughly three millimeters every year.

That slow burial has a consequence far larger than the marsh itself. Because the carbon in those dead plants gets locked away in the peat instead of returning to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide, the marsh acts as a genuine carbon sink. Our best estimate is that Cormorant Bend pulls down and stores on the order of 1.2 tonnes of carbon per hectare every single year. Multiply that across every hectare of healthy coastal marsh in the region and you begin to see why people have started calling these places blue carbon ecosystems and fighting so hard to protect them.

The lack of oxygen in the soil is not a defect, by the way. It is the engine that drives some of the marsh's most valuable chemistry. Just a centimeter or two below the surface the soil turns anoxic, and what a chemist would call the redox potential drops steeply into negative territory. In exactly that zone, specialized bacteria strip dissolved nitrogen straight out of the water through a process called denitrification, converting nitrate into ordinary, harmless nitrogen gas that simply bubbles away into the air. A marsh of this size can quietly remove a startling load of the fertilizer runoff that would otherwise pour into the bay and feed suffocating algae blooms.

There is a genuine tradeoff here, though, and I want to be honest about it rather than sell you a fairy tale. Those same waterlogged, oxygen starved soils that store all that carbon are also the perfect home for microbes that produce methane, which is a far more potent greenhouse gas than carbon dioxide over the short term. So a freshwater marsh is simultaneously a carbon sink through its peat and a methane source through its bacteria. Which effect ultimately wins out depends heavily on salinity. The saltier a marsh becomes, the more the sulfate in seawater suppresses those methane producing microbes, which is one reason salt marshes often come out looking better on the greenhouse ledger than freshwater ones.

Let me pull you back up out of the mud and into the living, breathing marsh, because it is spectacular at dawn. The signature sound at Cormorant Bend is the call of the banded marsh chorus frog, a little amphibian barely the size of your thumbnail, whose call sounds exactly like a fingernail dragged slowly down the teeth of a comb. On a still April morning a thousand of them calling together is genuinely deafening. They breed in the shallow warm pools that linger for a few weeks after the big spring tides, and their tadpoles have to race to grow legs before those pools dry down.

Beneath the surface, the mud itself is astonishingly alive. A single handful holds a whole community of macroinvertebrates, the amphipods and midge larvae and tiny snails that most visitors never notice. Those small animals are the hidden engine of the food web, because they shred and consume the enormous quantity of dead plant material, the detritus, that the sedge and cattail drop every autumn. In a marsh like this one the food web is built from the bottom up on decaying plants rather than on living green leaves, and that detritus based foundation is what makes these wetlands so extraordinarily productive.

All of that invertebrate life is, in turn, food for birds, and the marsh feeds an enormous number of them. The most secretive resident is the American bittern, a stocky heron that, when it feels threatened, freezes bolt upright with its bill pointed straight at the sky and simply vanishes against the vertical reeds. In the fall, tens of thousands of migrating ducks and sandpipers stage at Cormorant Bend, refueling on the fallen seeds and the fat rich invertebrates before they push on south for the winter. Lose the marsh and you do not just lose the frogs, you punch a hole in a flyway that stretches across a continent.

The tidal channels that thread through the marsh are nurseries in their own right. On a single net haul last June we counted more than four hundred juvenile salmon sheltering in one channel at Cormorant Bend, alongside sticklebacks and the little killifish we call mummichogs. Those young fish fatten on marsh insects for weeks, hiding from bigger predators in the shallow water, before they are large enough to survive out in the open bay.

We also keep a close eye on the water itself. Once a month a student walks a fixed transect across the marsh with a handheld sonde, logging the temperature, the salinity, and the dissolved oxygen at each numbered stake. It was that patient monthly record that first caught the oxygen crash of the summer of 2020, when a warm, still week drove the dissolved oxygen down low enough to leave the fish gasping at the surface.

And the marsh quietly protects the town behind it. When the big storm surge of 2021 pushed up the Alder estuary, the broad, shallow marsh soaked up the water and dragged its speed down, and the flood that finally reached the village was measurably lower than the engineers had feared. A healthy marsh is a seawall that rebuilds itself for free, every single tide.

No honest account of this place is complete without a warning, so here it is. An aggressive introduced strain of the common reed, Phragmites australis, has begun marching along the drier upland edge of the marsh. It grows in towering, ferociously dense monocultures that shade out and crowd the native sedge and cattail, and it offers far less food and shelter to wildlife in return. Once a stand of it is truly established, it is extraordinarily difficult and expensive to remove, so the cheapest victory is always to catch it early.

That threat brings me to the restoration story I most love to tell. Back in 2019 the old Alder Slough levee, an earthen dike that had cut the northern third of the marsh off from the tide for almost seventy years, was deliberately breached by the conservancy in three carefully placed cuts. The results came faster than anyone dared hope. Within two growing seasons the sedge had recolonized the reconnected flats on its own, juvenile salmon and stickleback were using the new tidal channels, and the bare subsided ground had begun trapping sediment and building back upward again. It is one of the clearest restoration success stories anywhere in the region.

I should tell you how we actually know the marsh is building upward, since I keep quoting that three millimeters a year. We measure it directly. We lay down a thin bright marker horizon of white feldspar clay on the marsh surface, then come back years later and cut a tiny core straight through it to see exactly how much fresh sediment has buried the layer. We pair that with elevation surveys using survey grade satellite positioning accurate to a few millimeters. It is slow, patient, muddy work, and it is the only way to answer the question that matters most.

And that question, the one that honestly keeps me awake at night, is sea level rise. Cormorant Bend can build itself upward at about three millimeters a year, as I said, but if the sea begins to rise faster than the marsh can accrete, the marsh will eventually drown in place, its plants stranded underwater too long to survive. Its only escape is to migrate slowly inland onto higher ground, and it can only do that if we have left it somewhere to go. Protecting that upland buffer, the belt of open low lying ground just above today's marsh, may honestly be the single most important thing we can do for Cormorant Bend over the next fifty years. Thank you for listening, and I would be glad to take your questions.
