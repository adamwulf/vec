Welcome back to the counter, everyone. Today I want to walk you through the sourdough flatbread I make almost every week, because it is forgiving, it is fast for a sourdough recipe, and it turns one jar of neglected starter into dinner. My starter is named Gus, he lives in a squat mason jar on the second shelf of the fridge, and he only gets fed when I remember, which is honestly part of why this recipe exists.

Let me give you the numbers first, because the ratios are the whole game here. I use three hundred grams of bread flour, two hundred and thirty five grams of water, which lands us right around seventy eight percent hydration, ninety grams of ripe starter, and eight grams of fine sea salt. That is a wet, slack dough on purpose. A wetter dough gives you those big irregular holes and the blistered char we are chasing, so do not panic when it feels sticky and alive under your hands.

I start by dissolving the starter into the water before the flour ever shows up. Swirl it until the water goes cloudy and a few bubbles rise, because that tells you Gus is awake and the wild yeast is actually going to lift this dough. Then I add the flour and mix with a stiff spatula until there is no dry flour left, just a shaggy, craggy mess. Cover it and walk away for thirty minutes. This rest is called the autolyse, and it lets the flour drink the water and start building gluten on its own, with zero effort from you.

After the autolyse I sprinkle the salt over the top with a splash of water and squish it in with wet fingers. Now we do the part people overthink: the stretch and fold. Reach under one side of the dough, pull it up until it almost tears, and fold it over the middle. Rotate the bowl a quarter turn and do it again, four folds total, maybe fifteen seconds of work. I repeat that set three times, spaced about forty five minutes apart, so the whole thing spans a lazy afternoon while you do other things.

Between folds the dough transforms. It goes from a torn, lumpy blob to something smooth and elastic that pulls back at you and holds a dome. That is the gluten organizing itself into a net strong enough to trap gas. By the third fold the dough should feel puffy, jiggly, and noticeably lighter, and you will see a scatter of bubbles rising to the surface right under the skin.

Then comes the bulk fermentation, which is just a fancy way of saying the long rise. I leave it covered on the counter for roughly four to six hours at normal room temperature, until it has grown by about half again its size and looks domed and full of life. If your kitchen is cold, this takes longer, and if it is a heat wave, keep a close eye, because a wet dough can go from perfect to overproofed and soupy faster than you expect. When in doubt, watch the dough, not the clock.

Once the bulk rise is done, I turn the dough out onto a well floured counter and divide it into six even pieces with a bench scraper. I gently cup each piece and drag it toward me on the unfloured part of the counter to build a little surface tension, until each one is a taut round ball. Cover them with a towel and let them rest for twenty minutes so the gluten relaxes and stops fighting you when you shape.

Here is where the flatbread part actually happens. I take one relaxed ball, dust it with flour, and press it out with my fingertips, then stretch it into a rough oval about a quarter inch thick. Do not use a rolling pin if you can help it, because a rolling pin crushes all those precious bubbles you worked so hard to build. You want an uneven, hand stretched shape with thick edges and a thinner middle, like a rustic naan.

The heat is everything from here. I get a cast iron skillet screaming hot, as hot as my stove will go, which is close to five hundred degrees at the surface, and I do not add any oil at first. I lay the stretched dough into the dry pan and let it sit undisturbed for about ninety seconds. Almost immediately you will see it start to dome and blister, with big bubbles pushing up across the top, and the underside going from pale to freckled to a deep leopard spotted brown.

When the top looks dry at the edges and the bottom is charred in spots, I flip it. The second side cooks faster, maybe forty five seconds to a minute, and if there is a stubborn flat spot I press it gently with a folded towel to encourage one last balloon of steam. That puff you get in the pan is the same steam that makes a pita pocket, so if you nail the heat, some of these will inflate like a hot air balloon and then settle back down soft.

The moment each flatbread comes off the heat, I brush it with a little olive oil, a scatter of flaky salt, and a heavy pinch of za'atar while it is still hot enough to bloom the spices. The oil carries the flavor into all those little craters and the residual heat toasts the sesame and thyme in the za'atar so the whole kitchen smells like a spice market. Stack them under a towel to keep them soft and steamy while you cook the rest.

A few troubleshooting notes before you go. If your flatbread is dense and gummy, your starter was probably sluggish, so feed Gus and wait until he doubles reliably before baking. If it tastes flat and boring, you likely under salted or rushed the bulk fermentation. And if it sticks and tears in the pan, your dough was too wet for your flour, so dial the water back to seventy percent next time and work your way up as your confidence grows.

That is the whole thing: one sleepy jar of starter, five ingredients, an afternoon of gentle folds, and a ripping hot pan. Serve these warm with hummus, tear them over a stew, or fold one around whatever is in the fridge. Thanks for hanging out at the counter with me, and I will see you next week.
