Welcome to Salt and Stone, the podcast about the lonely towers that kept sailors alive. Tonight's tower is Gannet Rock Light, a slim granite column that has stood on a bare tidal reef off a fictional stretch of the northern coast since the year eighteen fifty eight. If you have ever wondered what it took to keep a single flame burning through a winter gale, stay with me.

Gannet Rock rises one hundred and twenty feet from a rock that vanishes completely at high tide. That height was not vanity. From the gallery at the top, the light throws its beam nearly twenty nautical miles out to sea, far enough to warn a ship long before the reef could tear open its hull.

The real jewel of the tower was the lens. Gannet Rock was fitted with a first order Fresnel lens, a colossal beehive of glass standing taller than a person, assembled from over a thousand individually hand ground prisms. Each prism bends a stray sliver of lamplight and folds it into one concentrated, sweeping blade of light. A single oil flame, magnified this way, could be seen from the far horizon.

Every lighthouse spoke in its own rhythm, so a captain could tell them apart in the dark. Gannet Rock's signature was two quick flashes every fifteen seconds, a pattern printed on every chart of that coast. Sailors did not just see a light, they read it, the way you would recognize a friend's knock at the door.

For its first decades the light burned whale oil, and later kerosene, tended by a keeper who trimmed the wick through the night. Our records name a keeper called Ephraim Coyle, who supposedly kept the log for nineteen unbroken winters, hauling fuel up a spiral stair in the dark.

When fog swallowed the beam, light was useless, so the station answered with sound. A great fog horn moaned across the water every ninety seconds, a mournful note that sailors said they felt in their chests before they ever heard it.

Electricity finally reached the rock in nineteen thirty four, and the faithful oil lamp was retired. Automation came last, in nineteen eighty nine, when the final keeper rowed ashore for good and a machine took over the watch. The tower still flashes tonight, twice every fifteen seconds, talking to an empty sea.
