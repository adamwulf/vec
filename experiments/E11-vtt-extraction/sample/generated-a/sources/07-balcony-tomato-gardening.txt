If you have a sunny balcony and a stubborn belief that you cannot grow anything, this one is for you, because tomatoes in pots are the gateway plant. Let me walk you through growing a ridiculously sweet cherry tomato in a container, start to finish.

Pick the right variety first. For a balcony I always steer people toward Sungold, a cherry tomato that ripens to a glowing orange and tastes almost like candy. It is an indeterminate type, which means it is a vine that keeps growing and fruiting all season, so it will need something to climb.

Now the pot. Do not be shy about size, because a cramped root ball is the number one reason balcony tomatoes sulk. Use at least a five gallon container, and a fabric grow bag is even better than plastic, since it lets the roots breathe and stops them circling into a tangled knot.

Sun is non negotiable. A tomato wants a bare minimum of six hours of direct sun a day, and more is better, so put it in the brightest, hottest corner you have. If your balcony only gets dappled afternoon light, the plant will grow leggy and give you a handful of sad, pale fruit.

Watering is where most people go wrong. Containers dry out fast, so check daily by pushing a finger an inch into the soil, and water deeply whenever that top inch feels dry. The goal is steady, even moisture, because wild swings between bone dry and soaking are what trigger blossom end rot, that ugly black leathery patch on the bottom of the fruit.

That rot, by the way, is really a calcium problem caused by inconsistent watering, not a disease you can spray away. Keep the moisture even and mix a little crushed eggshell or garden lime into the soil at planting, and you will mostly dodge it.

Feed the plant every couple of weeks with a balanced liquid fertilizer once it starts flowering, because a fruiting vine in a pot is a hungry thing with nowhere else to find nutrients. And prune the little shoots, called suckers, that sprout in the crook between the main stem and a branch, so the plant pours its energy into fruit instead of endless leaves. Do that, and one plant can feed you cherry tomatoes all summer.
