You do not need a studio full of expensive strobes to shoot a gorgeous portrait. You need one window and a little understanding of how light behaves. Today I want to show you how to make flattering window light portraits with almost no gear at all.

Start by finding a north facing window, if you can. A window that never gets direct sun gives you soft, even light all day long, because the whole sky becomes your light source instead of one harsh point. That softness is what wraps gently around a face and hides skin texture instead of carving it up with hard shadows.

If your only window blasts direct sunlight, do not despair, just tape a white bed sheet or a sheer curtain over the glass. That thin fabric turns a tiny, hard sun into a big, soft panel, exactly like a professional softbox, for the price of doing laundry.

Now place your subject. Have them stand close to the glass and turn about forty five degrees toward it, not flat on and not fully sideways. That angle lets the light rake across the face, so one side is bright and the other falls into gentle shadow, giving the portrait shape and depth instead of a flat, flash lit look.

Watch the shadow the nose makes. When you get a small triangle of light on the shadowed cheek, just under the eye, that is the classic look called Rembrandt lighting. Turn the face a little more toward the window and that triangle opens into loop lighting, a softer, friendlier pattern. Learning to see that little triangle is half the game.

The dark side of the face will go too dark on its own, so bounce some light back in. Hold a big piece of white foam board just out of frame on the shadow side, and it kicks a soft fill back onto the face, opening up the shadows without adding a second light. A cheap dollar store foam board works beautifully.

Finally, the settings. Open your aperture wide, around f two point eight, to blur the background and let in plenty of that soft light, keep the ISO around four hundred, and get in close. And always, always focus on the eyes, because a portrait lives or dies on the catchlights, those little reflections of the window shining back at you from your subject's eyes.
