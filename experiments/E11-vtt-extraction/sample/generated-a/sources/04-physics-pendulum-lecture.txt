Good morning. Before we open the textbook, look at the front of the room, where I have hung a simple pendulum from the ceiling bracket: a solid brass bob on a length of fishing line exactly one meter long, measured from the pivot to the center of the bob. For the next hour this ordinary swinging weight is going to teach us more about oscillation than any chapter could, so keep your eyes on it while I talk.

Let me pull the bob to one side and release it. Notice that it does not just fall, it swings past the bottom, climbs the far side to almost the same height, and comes back. That trade is the heart of the whole system. At the top of each swing the bob is momentarily still, holding the most gravitational potential energy and zero kinetic energy. At the very bottom it is moving fastest, so all of that stored potential energy has become kinetic energy. Energy is sloshing back and forth between the two forms, and if there were no friction it would do that forever.

Now let us time it. I will count ten complete swings, where one complete swing means over and back to the starting side, and divide by ten. Watch the stopwatch. That gives us a period of almost exactly two seconds for this one meter pendulum. Hold onto that number, because in a moment we are going to predict it from theory and see how close we land.

Here is the governing equation for a simple pendulum, and I want you to write it down: the period T equals two pi times the square root of the length L divided by little g, the acceleration due to gravity. On the surface of the Earth, g is about nine point eight one meters per second squared. So plug in one meter for L, divide by nine point eight one, take the square root, multiply by two pi, and you get roughly two point zero zero six seconds. That is astonishingly close to what we just measured by hand, and the small discrepancy is mostly my thumb on the stopwatch.

Stare at that formula for a second, because it is telling you something almost unbelievable. The mass of the bob is nowhere in it. I could swap this brass weight for a paperclip or a bowling ball, and as long as the length stayed the same, the period would not change. Galileo noticed this centuries ago, supposedly while watching a lamp swing in a cathedral and timing it against his own pulse, and it is still one of the most counterintuitive results in introductory physics.

There is a catch, and this is the crucial fine print. The formula only holds when the swing is small, when the angle from vertical stays under about fifteen degrees. We say the amplitude θ must be < 15° for the nice clean answer to work. In that small angle regime the restoring force is very nearly proportional to the displacement, which is the exact condition for what we call simple harmonic motion. Push the pendulum out to a wild sixty degree swing, so that θ > 15°, and the real period grows a few percent longer than the formula predicts, so the tidy equation quietly breaks down.

Within that small angle limit, though, we get the beautiful property called isochronism. Watch closely as the swings get smaller and smaller from air resistance. The arc is shrinking, the bob travels less distance on each pass, yet each swing still takes the same amount of time. A big swing and a small swing share one period. That is why the pendulum became the beating heart of accurate clocks for nearly three hundred years, from the first pendulum clock in the sixteen hundreds until quartz crystals finally dethroned it.

Let me change one variable to prove the length relationship. If I want to double the period, from two seconds to four seconds, I do not double the length, I have to quadruple it, all the way out to four meters, because the period depends on the square root of the length. That square root is why grandfather clocks are so tall and why a child on a long chain swing drifts back and forth so lazily compared to a short one. Halve the length and the period drops by a factor of the square root of two, not by half.

Now, about that slow shrinking we watched. In the real world energy leaks out, mostly to air resistance and a little to friction at the pivot, so the amplitude decays over time. We call that damping. Our classroom pendulum is very lightly damped, so it takes many minutes to wind down. If I dunked the bob in a tub of honey it would be heavily damped and might not even complete one full swing, just crawl back to center and stop. Real clocks fight this decay by feeding in a tiny push each cycle from a wound spring or a hanging weight, exactly replacing the lost energy.

One more idea, and it is a big one. Everything so far assumed all the mass sits at a single point on a weightless string. That is the idealized simple pendulum. A real swinging object, like a bat hanging by its handle or a swinging door, is a physical pendulum, and its period depends on how its mass is distributed around the pivot, a quantity called the moment of inertia. The physics is richer, but the same energy trade between potential and kinetic still drives the motion.

Finally, look up at the ceiling where I have marked the swing plane with tape. If we let a very heavy pendulum on a very long wire swing for hours, the plane of its swing appears to slowly rotate over the course of a day. The pendulum is not turning, the Earth is turning underneath it. That famous demonstration is called a Foucault pendulum, and it is one of the few experiments you can do indoors that lets you directly see the planet spinning beneath your feet.

So here is the whole lecture in one breath. Energy trades between potential and kinetic on every swing. The period follows two pi root L over g, ignores the mass entirely, and only stays that simple while the angle stays under fifteen degrees. Length matters as a square root, damping steals amplitude over time, and a long enough pendulum will quietly reveal the rotation of the Earth. Not bad for a brass weight on a string.
